Problem Questions: Do’s and don’ts 
1. Do not start by summarising the facts of the 
problem. Never a need to rehearse the facts of a 
case unless you are constructing an analogy.
2. Do not start with an introduction to the broad 
area of law. You have limited time and/or 
limited word count!
3. Do start by identifying the issues raised by the 
facts, 
then 
apply 
relevant 
authorities 
accordingly.
4. Do identify all the issues 
 5. Do refer to the principles/relevant authorities. 
6. Do set out your reasoning.
7. Where the facts suggest two equally viable 
possibilities, do consider both. Be led by the facts, but 
do not speculate wildly or make up facts. 
8. Do not discuss issues that are not raised by the 
problem. 
Problem Questions: Do’s and don’ts 
 9. Do not write everything you know about a 
topic. You must relate everything you write to 
the problem question.
10. Do use separate paragraphs for each issue. 
An 
issue 
requiring 
extensive 
discussion 
will generally need more than one paragraph. 
11.  Do make it clear when you have ﬁnished 
with an issue and are moving onto the next.  Use 
headings.
 
12. Do apply the principles/authorities to the facts of 
the problem:
i.
You must analyse the facts in light of the 
authorities and apply the principles/ 
authorities to the facts in the problem.
ii.
Remember you need analysis and application 
before you can conclude.
13. Do not forget to set out your conclusion, and 
ensure that it matches the problem instructions i.e. are 
you answering the question?

3.
Factual causation
• ‘But-for’ test; material contribution to injury; material contribution to risk of injury; loss of a chance
4.
Legal causation
• Remoteness of damage; new intervening acts; supervening events; ‘the scope of duty’ principle; 
coincidental harms
LAW OF TORT
Duty of care recap
The approach to determining a duty of care after Robinson:
1. Does the case fall within an “established category” of 
DoC? 
2. If the case is a novel one, does coherence with analogous 
cases imply that there should be a DoC?
3. If coherence with analogous cases does not clearly imply a 
duty/no duty, then do ‘policy reasons’ imply there ought 
to be a duty? [This is where Caparo comes in.]
LAW OF TORT
Duty of care recap
What is an established category?
Where D does an act which reasonably foreseeably causes 
physical injury or property damage there is always a duty of 
care.
Things are more complicated for: 
Pure economic loss – Hedley Byrne 
Omissions – N v Poole BC
Pure psychiatric injury 
LAW OF TORT
Duty of care recap
Novel duties – ABC v St George’s
D treating C and dad. Ds learn info about C from dad which 
harmed C when not disclosed.
Policy reasoning: close proximity b/c both C and dad in 
treatment together; therapist’s duty of conﬁdence is not 
absolute; would not open ﬂoodgates.
Novel duty: D must  balance C’s interest in being informed of 
her genetic risk against dad’s interest in preserving 
conﬁdentiality.
