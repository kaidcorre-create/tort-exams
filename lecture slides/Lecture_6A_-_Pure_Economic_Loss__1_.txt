LAW OF TORT
Pure economic loss (‘PEL’)
Easy to show DoC in cases of an act which reasonably 
foreseeably causes physical injury or property damage
Harder to show DoC in cases of PEL, pure psychiatric 
injury, or omissions – ‘special duty problems’
Something more needed in PEL cases to create a DoC: 
normally this is an “assumption of responsibility”
LAW OF TORT
Justiﬁcations for the 
exclusionary rule
Economic loss lower in the hierarchy of interests than 
physical injury/property damage
Floodgates: judges do not want to create “liability in an 
indeterminate amount for an indeterminate time to an 
indeterminate class” (Cadozo CJ in Ultramares) 
Liability would often be disproportionate to the wrong
Important to protect freedom of economic activity
Simple rules create legal certainty/predictability
LAW OF TORT
Justiﬁcations for the 
exclusionary rule
“One of the most important distinctions always to be 
observed lies in the law's essentially diﬀerent approach to the 
diﬀerent kinds of damage which one party may have 
suﬀered in consequence of the acts or omissions of another. It 
is one thing to owe a duty of care to avoid causing injury to 
the person or property of others. It is quite another to avoid 
causing others to suﬀer purely economic loss.”
(Lord Bridge, Caparo v Dickman)

LAW OF TORT
Spartan Steel v Martin 
& Co [1973] 1 QB 27 
Facts:
D negligently damaged a power cable owned by T that was critical to 
C’s factory, leaving C without power for 14 hours
3 kinds of damage: 
(i) damage to steel in furnace at time of power cut 
which had 
to be poured away; 
(ii) loss of proﬁt from the sale of that steel; and 
(iii) loss of proﬁt from other steel which could have been 
made 
during the rest of the 14 hours.
LAW OF TORT
Spartan Steel v Martin 
& Co [1973] 1 QB 27 
Held:
C can recover for damage types (i) and (ii), but not for 
(iii)
(i) was damage to property
(ii) was economic loss consequent on property damage
(iii) was pure economic loss
LAW OF TORT
Spartan Steel v Martin 
& Co [1973] 1 QB 27 
“the cutting of the supply of electricity … is a hazard which we all run. … Such a 
hazard is regarded by most people as a thing they must put up with—without seeking 
compensation from anyone. … When the supply is cut oﬀ, they do not go running 
round to their solicitor. … They just put up with it. They try to make up the economic 
loss by doing more work next day. This is a healthy attitude which the law should 
encourage.”
“if claims for economic loss were permitted for this particular hazard, there would be 
no end of claims.”
“the risk of economic loss should be suﬀered by the whole community who suﬀer the 
losses—usually many but comparatively small losses—rather than on the one pair of 
shoulders, that is, on the contractor on whom the total of them, all added together, 
might be very heavy.” 
(Denning LJ, p.38)
LAW OF TORT
Murphy v Brentwood DC 
[1991] 1 AC 398 
Facts:
D approved building plans for house purchased by C
The foundations of the house cracked, causing damage to 
the walls and pipes of the house
Held:
D not liable – pure economic loss
LAW OF TORT
Murphy v Brentwood DC 
[1991] 1 AC 398 
“there is no liability in tort upon a manufacturer towards the purchaser from a 
retailer of an article which turns out to be useless or valueless through defects 
due to careless manufacture. The loss is economic.” (Lord Keith, p.465)
“if such a duty is incumbent upon the local authority, a similar duty must 
necessarily be incumbent also upon the builder of the house. If the builder of 
the house is to be so subject, there can be grounds in logic or in principle for 
not extending liability upon like grounds to the manufacturer of a chattel. 
That would open on an exceedingly wide ﬁeld of claims. …” (Lord Keith, 
p.469)
“it is necessary to search for and ﬁnd something more than the mere 
reasonable foreseeability of damage …[to provide] the degree of ‘proximity’ 
necessary” (Lord Oliver, p.486)

2. Assumption of 
responsibility 
LAW OF TORT
The exception: assumption of 
responsibility
To establish that that D owes C a duty of care for pure 
economic loss, C must usually show that:
there was a relationship of trust and conﬁdence between D 
and C;
that D voluntarily assumed responsibility for the risk to C; 
and 
that in negligent misstatement cases, C reasonably relied 
on D’s advice or information.
LAW OF TORT
Hedley Byrne v Heller 
[1964] AC 465 
Facts:
C wanted to know if T was creditworthy
D (T’s bank) negligently provided a reference for C, saying 
T was creditworthy
D clariﬁed that references were being given ‘without 
responsibility’ 
T went bankrupt, and C made a loss as a result
LAW OF TORT
Hedley Byrne v Heller 
[1964] AC 465 
Held:
D owed C a duty of care in relation to the PEL
BUT … the disclaimer – that references were being given 
‘without responsibility’ – meant D was not liable in the 
end
LAW OF TORT
Hedley Byrne v Heller 
[1964] AC 465 
Justiﬁcation 
“A reasonable man, knowing that he was being trusted or that his skill 
and judgment were being relied on, would, I think, have three courses 
open to him. [1] He could keep silent or decline to give the information 
or advice sought: or [2] he could give an answer with a clear 
qualiﬁcation that he accepted no responsibility for it …: or [3] he could 
simply answer without any such qualiﬁcation. If he chooses to adopt 
the last course he must, I think, be held to have accepted some 
responsibility for his answer being given carefully, or to have accepted 
a relation ship with the inquirer which requires him to exercise such 
care as the circumstances require.” (Lord Reid, p.486) 
LAW OF TORT
Hedley Byrne v Heller 
[1964] AC 465 
Justiﬁcation 
“the categories of special relationships which may give rise 
to a duty to take care … are not limited to contractual 
relationships … but include also relationships which …  are 
“equivalent to contract”, that is, where there is an 
assumption of responsibility in circumstances in which, but 
for the absence of consideration, there would be a contract.” 
(Lord Devlin, p.528-529)

A. Extension to third

Smith v Eric S Bush (a ﬁrm) [1990] 1 AC 831
Facts:

Cs were purchasers of properties 

They paid their mortgagees for a valuation of the properties

The mortgagees’ valuers, D, negligently overvalued the properties

Cs didn’t get their own further survey and suﬀered loss when the valuations each turned out to be 
negligent 
Held:

D owed a DoC to C in respect of the PEL, even though C‘s relationship was only with their mortgagee, 
not with D
LAW OF TORT
A. Extension to third 
parties
Smith v Eric S Bush (a ﬁrm) [1990] 1 AC 831
“In the present appeals, the relationship between the valuer 
and the purchaser is “akin to contract.” The valuer knows 
that the consideration which he receives derives from the 
purchaser and is passed on by the mortgagee, and the valuer 
also knows that the valuation will determine whether or not 
the purchaser buys the house.” 
(Lord Templeman, p.846)
LAW OF TORT
A. Extension to third 
parties
Smith v Eric S Bush (a ﬁrm) [1990] 1 AC 831
“The four critical facts are that [D] knew from the outset: 
(1) that the report would be shown to [C]; 
(2) that [C] would probably rely on the valuation contained therein in deciding 
whether to buy the house without obtaining an independent valuation; 
(3) that if, in these circumstances, the valuation was, having regard to the actual 
condition of the house, excessive, [C] would be likely to suﬀer loss; and 
(4) that [C] had paid to the building society a sum to defray [D’s] fee.”
(Lord Jauncey, p.871 )

“although Hedley Byrne itself was concerned with the

principle in the case is not so limited and extends to include

(Lord Goﬀ in Spring v Guardian Assurance [1995] 2 AC 296, 
p.318)

Henderson v Merrett Syndicates Ltd [1995] 2 AC 145 
Facts: 
Cs invested money in the Lloyds of London insurance market and 
lost it as a result of Ds’ alleged negligence in managing their 
aﬀairs
Held:
Ds owed a duty of care on the basis of their assumption of 
responsibility 
LAW OF TORT
B. Extension to the 
provision of services
Subjective reliance not necessary in provision of services cases?
“where [C] entrusts the defendant with the conduct of his aﬀairs, in general or in 
particular, the defendant may be held to have assumed responsibility to [C], and 
[C] to have relied on the defendant to exercise due skill and care, in respect of 
such conduct.” (Lord Goﬀ in Spring v Guardian Assurance [1995] 2 AC 296, p.318)
“although, in the case of the provision of information and advice, reliance upon it 
by the other party will be necessary to establish a cause of action … nevertheless 
there may be other circumstances in which there will be the necessary reliance … 
In particular, as cases concerned with solicitor and client demonstrate, where [C] 
entrusts the defendant with the conduct of his aﬀairs, in general or in 
particular, he may be held to have relied on the defendant to exercise due skill 
and care in such conduct.” (Lord Goﬀ in Henderson, p.180)
