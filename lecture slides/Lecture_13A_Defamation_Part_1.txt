Defamation Act 2013:
some notable interventions
- Clearer guidance with ‘serious harm’ 
- Introduced the ‘public interest’ defence (s. 4)
- Replaced the ‘honest or fair comment’ defence 
with ‘honest opinion’ (s. 3)
- Further protection for ‘distributors,’ including 
websites, alongside court ordered removal (s. 
13)of material (various sections)
- Some technical certainty: heard by judges, limit 
jurisdictional scope.

Elements of a defamation 
action
(Defamation Act + precedent) 
• (1) defamatory statement
• (2) statement must be such that it  has caused/ 
is likely to cause serious harm to reputation 
OR, in the case of a trading for profit, has 
caused or likely to cause serious financial loss 
(see s 1 Defamation Act 2013)
• (3) reference to that other person
• (4) publication
‘a threshold requirement of seriousness’

2. Requirement of 
serious harm
Section 1, Defamation Act 2013 
1. A statement is not defamatory unless its publication has 
caused or is likely to cause serious harm to the reputation of 
the claimant. 
2. For the purposes of this section, harm to the reputation of a 
body that trades for profit is not “serious harm” unless it has 
caused or is likely to cause the body serious financial loss.
Designed to prevent frivolous actions
10
Clarity on ‘Serious Harm’ s.1(1)
Lachaux v Independent Print Ltd [2019] UKSC 27 (SC)
• i) the scale of the publications; 
• (ii) the fact that the statements complained of had 
come to the attention of at least one identifiable 
person in the United Kingdom who knew Mr Lachaux;
• (iii) that they were likely to have come to the attention 
of others who either knew him or would come to know 
him in future; and 
• (iv) the gravity of the statements themselves, 
according to the meaning attributed to them…
• - Per  Warby J

3. Reference to the 
Claimant
• Group defamation

Knupffer v London Express Newspaper Ltd [1944] 
•
Referring a group as Nazi sympathisers; 24 
UK members, but topic on US/France

If a class of persons is defamed, individual belonging to 
that class will only be able to succeed in defamation

•
They can prove that it was capable of referring to them

•
AND, that the statement was actually understood by people to

13
4. Publication of the 
statement 
•
Statement considered as published if made available to at least 
one person other than the claimant 
•
Where the claimant has NOT made the statement available to 
a third party, will not be considered as ‘published’; Hinderer 
v Cole [1977]
•
Letter addressed to the claimant and opened by an 
unauthorised third party not published; Huth v Huth [1915]
•
‘Serious harm’ requirement outlined in s. 1 Defamation Act 
2013 tends to weigh against, though does not in itself 
prevent, an action going forward where it is based upon 
publication to just one other person.
14
Subsequent Publications 
• REPUBLICATION  and ‘single publication 
rule’ (s. 8): Defamation Act 2013 -unless 
the republication is “materially different” 
from the first publication
• REPETITION of a defamatory statement = 
new publication = a new cause of action
• An action can be taken against anyone 
who has repeated the allegation as well 
as the party which issued the original 
defamatory statement
