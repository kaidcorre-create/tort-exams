LAW OF TORT
1. Acts vs Omissions
LAW OF TORT
Acts v omissions
“the distinction between acts and omissions is [not] meaningless or 
illogical. One must have regard to the purpose of the distinction as it is 
used in the law of negligence, which is to distinguish between regulating 
the way in which an activity may be conducted and imposing a duty to act 
upon a person who is not carrying on any relevant activity.” (Lord 
Hoﬀmann, p.945)
“The distinction between liability for acts and liability for omissions is … 
not free from controversy. In some cases the distinction is not clear cut. The 
categorisation may depend upon how broadly one looks when deciding 
whether the omission is a 'pure' omission or is part of a larger course of 
activity set in motion by the defendant.” (Lord Nicholls, p.930)
(Stovin v Wise [1996] AC 923)
LAW OF TORT
Example of acts v omissions
Rushbond Plc v JS Design Partnership LLP [2021] EWCA 
Civ 1889
D, an architect, visited C’s building and left door unlocked 
and alarm deactivated 
Burglar got in and started ﬁre
High Court: this was an omission case
Court of Appeal: this was an act case
LAW OF TORT
The exclusionary rule
“under our law two persons can stand aside and watch a third 
jump to his death: there is no legal duty to rescue.” (Schiemann 
LJ, Vellino v CC of Greater Machester [2001] EWCA Civ 1249, 
[13])
“The very parable of the good Samaritan…illustrates, in the 
conduct of the priest and of the Levite who passed by on the 
other side, an omission which was likely to have, as its 
reasonable and probable consequence, damage to the health of 
the victim of the thieves but for which the priest and the Levite 
would have incurred no civil liability in English law.” (Lord 
Diplock, Home Oﬃce v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd, p.1177)
LAW OF TORT
Justiﬁcation for the 
exclusionary rule
“One can put the matter in political, moral or economic terms. In political terms it is less of an 
invasion of an individual's freedom for the law to require him to consider the safety of others in 
his actions than to impose upon him a duty to rescue or protect. A moral version of this point 
may be called the “why pick on me?” argument. A duty to prevent harm to others or to render 
assistance to a person in danger or distress may apply to a large and indeterminate class of 
people who happen to be able to do something. Why should one be held liable rather than 
another? In economic terms, the eﬃcient allocation of resources usually requires an activity 
should bear its own costs. If it beneﬁts from being able to impose some of its costs on other 
people (what economists call “externalities”,) the market is distorted because the activity 
appears cheaper than it really is. So liability to pay compensation for loss caused by negligent 
conduct acts as a deterrent against increasing the cost of the activity to the community and 
reduces externalities. But there is no similar justiﬁcation for requiring a person who is not 
doing anything to spend money on behalf of someone else.”
(Lord Hoﬀmann, Stovin v Wise, p.943-944)
LAW OF TORT
Justiﬁcation for the 
exclusionary rule
1. Why pick on me?
• Strong where D is just one of a large class of people who could have helped C, but C 
chooses to sue D
• Weaker where D is the only person who can help C
2. Freedom/personal autonomy
• Forcing someone to act is much more intrusive than forcing them not to act
3. Legal certainty
• If there were duties to rescue, we would get into very diﬃcult questions about how 
serious and imminent that danger needs to be for a duty to be to be imposed
• Current law is relatively simple – no duty for omissions outside special circumstances
LAW OF TORT
Justiﬁcation for the 
exclusionary rule
4. Value of altruism
• It is valuable for people to choose whether to help others in need
• If the law forced people to help others, we would lose the value of making the purely 
moral choice
5. Absence of a right
• Arguably no moral obligation to help others – helping others is ‘supererogatory’ not 
obligatory
• N.b. this is a broadly ‘Kantian’ position – utilitarians might disagree with this
6. Individual responsibility
• In a case where a TP or C themselves is responsible for the situation, it would erode 
individual responsibility to make a bystander liable

2. Exceptions to The 
Exclusionary Rule
LAW OF TORT
The exceptions
“In the tort of negligence, a person A is not under a duty to take care to prevent 
harm occurring to person B through a source of danger not created by A unless: 
A.
A has assumed a responsibility to protect B from that danger, 
B.
A has done something which prevents another from protecting B from that 
danger, 
C.
A has a special level of control over that source of danger, or 
D.
A’s status creates an obligation to protect B from that danger.”
(Tofaris and Steel, ‘Negligence Liability for Omissions and the Police’ – aﬃrmed 
in Robinson [34] and N v Poole [76])
LAW OF TORT
Other ways of 
categorising exceptions
(1) creation of the risk, (2) special relationship, (3) 
occupier’s liability, (4) assumption of responsibility (Lord 
Scott, Mitchell v Glasgow County Council [2009] UKHL 11, 
[40])
(1) control, (2) assumption of responsibility, (3) creation (or 
adoption) of risks (H&R, p.85)
(1) special relationship between D and C, (2) special 
relationship between D and TP, (3) creation of source of 
danger, (4) failure to abate known danger (Lord Goﬀ, Smith 
v Littlewoods)
LAW OF TORT
Creation of risk: treating 
omissions as acts
E.g. a driver’s failure to break will be treated as an act
E.g. a motorist who has to leave his vehicle must warn others
“One must have regard to the purpose of the distinction as it is used in the 
law of negligence, which is to distinguish between 
[i] regulating the way in which an activity may be conducted and 
[ii] imposing a duty to act upon a person who is not carrying on 
any relevant activity.” 
(Lord Hoﬀmann, Stovin v Wise, p.945)
LAW OF TORT
Creation of risk: treating 
omissions as acts
Dorset Yacht Co Ltd v Home Oﬃce [1970] AC 1004
Prison oﬃcers brought young oﬀenders to an island and then left them 
unsupervised.
Reasonably foreseeable that they would attempt to escape and cause 
damage to property.
“All members of the House plainly did not regard the [Dorset Yacht] 
case as one in which the alleged breach of duty was merely an 
omission. … The negligence was caused by something which [D] did, 
namely to use their statutory powers of custody to bring the trainees 
onto the island, where they constituted a foreseeable risk to boat 
owners, and then take no care to prevent them escaping in the night.” 
(Lord Hoﬀmann, Stovin v Wise, p.948)
LAW OF TORT
Creation of risk: treating 
omissions as acts
Haynes v Harwood [1935] 1 KB 146
D left horses untethered on a busy street
They bolted when kids threw stones at them
C was injured
Held: D was liable as he had created the risk
LAW OF TORT
Creation of risk: treating 
omissions as acts
“Dorset Yacht and Haynes v Harwood are examples of cases 
where [D] acted in such a way as to create a risk of injury to 
others: in other words, they are not, on analysis, ‘omission’ 
cases at all, but cases where [D] undertook a positive activity 
which carried the risk of causing damage to others.” 
(Lord Reed, in Mitchell v Glasgow CC [2008] SC 351, [97])
LAW OF TORT
Creation of risk: treating 
omissions as acts
Topp v London Country Bus [1993] 1 WLR 976
D left bus with keys in ignition
TP stole bus and killed C
Held: D not liable
LAW OF TORT
A. Assumption of 
responsibility to C
“The assumption of responsibility is an undertaking that 
reasonable care will be taken, either express or more 
commonly implied, usually from the reasonable 
foreseeability of reliance on the exercise of such care. Thus … 
a hospital undertakes to exercise reasonable care in the 
medical treatment of its patients. The same is true, mutatis 
mutandis, of an education authority accepting pupils into its 
schools.” 
(Lord Reed, N v Poole BC, [80])
LAW OF TORT
A. Assumption of 
responsibility to C
Barrett v MOD [1995] 1 WLR 1217
Facts:
C was an oﬃcer on a naval base
He became very drunk, collapsed and was put to bed
No one stayed watch over C
C choked on his own vomit and died
LAW OF TORT
A. Assumption of 
responsibility to C 
Barrett v MOD [1995] 1 WLR 1217
Held:
“I would reverse the judge's ﬁnding that the defendant was under a duty to 
take reasonable care to prevent the deceased from abusing alcohol to the 
extent he did. Until he collapsed, I would hold that the deceased was in law 
alone responsible for his condition. Thereafter, when the defendant assumed 
responsibility for him, it accepts that the measures taken fell short of the 
standard reasonably to be expected. It did not summon medical assistance and 
its supervision of him was inadequate.” (Beldam LJ, p.1225)
N.b. can also be understood as category B case – by taking him to bed, D cut C 
oﬀ from sources of support which could have ensured he did not die during 
the night 
LAW OF TORT
A. Assumption of 
responsibility to C 
Costello v CC of Northumbria [1999] 1 All ER 550
Facts:
C, a police oﬃcer attacked in cell. Fellow oﬃcers failed to come to her 
aid.
Held:
Police oﬃcers assume a responsibility to one another to ‘watch each 
other’s back’. 
N.b. can also be understood as a category D case – because oﬃcers owe 
a duty to one another in virtue of their status
LAW OF TORT
A. Assumption of 
responsibility to C 
Swinney v CC of Northumbria [1997] QB 464
Facts:
C was a police informant
Documents including C’s name were stolen from a police car
C was then threatened by the person they had informed on
Held:
D had assumed responsibility to ensure C’s identity was not revealed 
unnecessarily

LAW OF TORT
B. Preventing another 
from protecting C 
Kent v Griﬃths [2001] QB 36
Facts:

C suﬀered an asthma attack.

When C’s husband called ambulance, he was told that an ambulance was well on the way and should 
arrive in seven or eight minutes. 

It did not arrive for 40 minutes. 

If C had been told that it would take the ambulance service 40 minutes to come, she would have driven 
to hospital. 
Held:

D owed a duty to C, in party because D’s act prevented C’s husband from driving her to the hospital.
LAW OF TORT
B. Preventing another 
from protecting C 
Tindall v CC of Thames Valley Police [2024] UKSC 33
Facts:
T crashed because of some ice on the road, but was ﬁne.
T was warning other motorists about the ice, but the police (D) arrived and took T away.
Later, C crashed and died because of the ice.
Held:
The police did make matters worse.
However, no liability because the police did not know that T had been warning other 
motorists about the ice
LAW OF TORT
C. Control over source of 
danger
Yetkin v Mahmood [2010] EWCA Civ 776
Facts:
D, a highway authority, had planted various shrubs, bushes and plants on central 
reservation. They failed to keep them trimmed.
C stepped out from central reservation without waiting for the lights to change in 
her favour. Her view of traﬃc was obscured by overgrown plants. C was hit by a 
car.
Held:
“This highway authority owed a duty to all road users … to use reasonable care in 
the manner in which it exercised its powers when it created and maintained the 
crossing facility.” (Smith LJ, [33])
LAW OF TORT
C. Control over source of 
danger
Sumner v Colborne [2018] EWCA Civ 1006
Facts:
C was a cyclist hit by a car. 
Visibility at the junction was severely restricted by the presence of vegetation on land next to the 
highway, for which D, a county council, had responsibility.
Held:
“Although work of cutting back the vegetation was included … in the Council’s maintenance 
operations, in no sense could the obstruction to visibility at the time of the accident be said to 
have been the result of a positive act by the Council. The most that could be said against the 
Council is that it failed to cut back the vegetation so as to prevent it from obstructing visibility at 
the material time. … [I]t is clear … that such a failure does not give rise to a liability in 
negligence; and that position is not aﬀected by the fact that the Council had carried out work of 
maintenance on the vegetation in the past” (Richards J, [23])
LAW OF TORT
C. Control over source of 
danger
Reeves v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis [2000] 1 AC 360 
Facts:
C’s partner was in police custody.
A doctor had found him to be of sound mind, but he committed suicide.
Held:
The police have a duty of care to take reasonable steps to assess whether prisoners pose a 
suicide risk. 
D was 50% responsible for the suicide.
N.b. can also be understood as a case in category B – by taking C into custody, the police cut 
him oﬀ from sources of support which might have helped him overcome suicidal 
tendencies.

Gower v LB Bromley [1999] ELR 356
“A headteacher and teachers have a duty to take such care of 
pupils in their charge as a careful parent would have in like

protect their well-being … The duty is to exercise the skill

(Auld LJ, [359])

LAW OF TORT
Summary
Diﬃcult to establish a DoC for a ‘pure’ omission
Where D creates the dangerous situation, then there is not 
a ‘pure’ omission
In cases of a ‘pure’ omission, D will only owe C a DoC if 
one of the four exceptions mentioned in N v Poole BC 
applies
The four exceptions often overlap and are not neatly 
delineated
