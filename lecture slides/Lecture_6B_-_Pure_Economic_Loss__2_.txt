LAW OF TORT
A. Professional not social 
context
“It is perhaps understandable that professional persons are taken to 
assume responsibility for economic loss to their clients. Typically, 
they give advice, prepare reports, draw up accounts, produce plans 
and so forth. They expect their clients and possibly others to act in 
reliance upon their work product, often with ﬁnancial or economic 
consequences. When one moves beyond the realm of professional 
retainers, it by no means follows that every contracting party 
assumes responsibilities (in the Hedley Byrne sense) to the other 
parties co-extensive with the contractual obligations. Such an 
analysis would be nonsensical.” 
(Jackson LJ in Robinson v Jones [2011] EWCA Civ 9, [75-76])
LAW OF TORT
A. Professional not social 
context
Burgess v Lejonvarn [2017] EWCA Civ 254
Facts:

Cs asked their friend and neighbour D, a qualiﬁed architect, to oversee the landscaping of their back 
garden. 

D was heavily involved in both organisation of contractors and delivery of the project, as well as in 
planning later elements. 

No payment had been discussed between the parties. 

Cs were unhappy with the quality of the work and because the costs had substantially overrun. 
Held:

This was a professional context – D owed C a DoC in relation to PEL
LAW OF TORT
A. Professional not social 
context
Burgess v Lejonvarn [2017] EWCA Civ 254
“For the Garden Project to be successful it was recognised by 
both Mrs Lejonvarn and the Burgesses that her professional 
services would be required. They were not services that could 
be provided by the builders. Although the Burgesses were not 
Mrs Lejonvarn’s clients in the contractual sense, on the judge’s 
ﬁndings both parties regarded the Burgesses as being her 
professional clients, and that was how Mrs Lejonvarn herself 
described them.”
(Hamblen LJ, [83])
LAW OF TORT
A. Professional not social 
context
Burgess v Lejonvarn [2017] EWCA Civ 254
“… the context was a professional one. It was not informal or 
social. There was an obvious relationship of proximity. Although 
she was not going to be paid initially the expectation was that she 
would be paid for later work. She held herself out as having 
professional skills. She said she would perform professional 
services and did so. She was aware that the Burgesses would be 
relying upon her to properly perform those services and it was 
foreseeable that economic loss would be caused to them if she did 
not.”
(Hamblen LJ, [86])
LAW OF TORT
B. D must assume 
responsibility to C 
personally
BNL v Playboy Club [2018] UKSC 43
Facts:

C, through its agent, T, sought a credit reference for Mr Barakat to gamble

D negligently gave a positive reference to T

D did not know that the reference was for C’s use

Mr Barakat was not creditworthy, and C lost money
Held:

D was not liable because D did not know the purpose of their reference 
LAW OF TORT
B. D must assume 
responsibility to C 
personally
BNL v Playboy Club [2018] UKSC 43
“where a statement is relied upon by B to whom A has passed it on, the 
representor owes no duty to B unless he knew that the statement was 
likely to be communicated to B. … [T]he representor must not only 
know that the statement is likely to be communicated to and relied 
upon by B. It must also be part of the statement’s known purpose that 
it should be communicated and relied upon by B, if the representor is 
to be taken to assume responsibility to B.” 
(Lord Sumption, [11])
LAW OF TORT
C. Reliance necessary in 
misstatement cases
We saw yesterday that reliance is no longer necessary in 
provision of services cases – see Henderson v Merrett
But it is still necessary in misstatement cases
“[D] will not assume responsibility towards [C] unless it 
was reasonable for the latter to have relied on what [D] 
said and unless [D] should reasonably have foreseen that 
[C] would do so. These are, as I have shown, two 
ingredients of the general liability in tort for negligent 
misrepresentation”. (Lord Wilson, NRAM v Steel [2018] 
UKSC 13, [32])
LAW OF TORT
D. PEL outside of assumption of 
responsibility? 
“although it may be decisive in many situations, the presence or absence of a 
voluntary assumption of responsibility does not necessarily provide the 
answer in all cases” (Lord Rodger, [20])
“it is correct to regard an assumption of responsibility as a suﬃcient but not a 
necessary condition of liability, a ﬁrst test which, if answered positively, may 
obviate the need for further inquiry. If answered negatively, further 
consideration is called for.” (Lord Bingham, [4])
(Customs and Excise Commissioners v Barclays Bank plc [2006] UKHL 28)
N.b. some of the cases where D was liable for PEL seem to lack the basic 
features of assumption of responsibility – e.g. White v Jones

LAW OF TORT
A. Reasonable reliance
Reeman v Department of Transport [1997] EWCA Civ 1355
D negligently issued certiﬁcate of seaworthiness for a ship
Certiﬁcates were issued for the purpose of safety at sea, 
not for the purposes of ensuring C did not make an 
economic loss
D had no DoC for PEL
Similar to Caparo: statements made for purpose X, cannot 
reasonably be relied on for very diﬀerent purpose Y
LAW OF TORT
A. Reasonable reliance
NRAM v Steel [2018] UKSC 13
Facts:
C loaned money to T as part of C’s business as a commercial lender. 
During dealings between C and T, C suﬀered PEL as a result of acting on 
negligent information in an email sent to them by D (T’s solicitors). 
C did not check the accuracy of the information themselves. 
Held:
No DoC because reliance not reasonable in the circumstances
LAW OF TORT
A. Reasonable reliance
NRAM v Steel [2018] UKSC 13
“We should accept that a commercial lender about to 
implement an agreement with its borrower referable to its 
security does not act reasonably if it proceeds upon no more 
than a description of its terms put forward by or on behalf of 
the borrower. … No authority has been cited to the court, nor 
discovered by me in preparing this judgment, in which it has 
been held that there was an assumption of responsibility for a 
careless misrepresentation about a fact wholly within the 
knowledge of the representee.” (Lord Wilson, [38])
LAW OF TORT
A. Reasonable reliance
Scullion v Bank of Scotland [2011] EWCA Civ 693
Negligent valuation of property led to losses for 
commercial by-to-let purchaser
Essentially commercial nature of the relationship meant 
there was no reasonable reliance on the bank’s survey 
report
Contrast with Smith v Eric Bush, where the relationship 
was between a professional and a normal purchaser who 
would live in the house
LAW OF TORT
A. Reasonable reliance
So, at least in misstatement cases:
If it was reasonable for C to rely on D’s statement, there will be a 
DoC
If it was unreasonable for C to rely on D’s statement, there will be no 
DoC
“The test is not simply reliance in fact. The test is whether [C] 
could reasonably rely on an assumption of personal responsibility by 
the individual who performed the services on behalf of the 
company…” (Lord Steyn, Williams v Natural Life Health Foods, p.837)
LAW OF TORT
B. An objective test
“The touchstone of liability is not the state of mind of the 
defendant. An objective test means that the primary focus must 
be on things said or done by the defendant or on his behalf in 
dealings with the plaintiﬀ. Obviously, the impact of what a 
defendant says or does must be judged in the light of the relevant 
contextual scene. Subject to this qualiﬁcation the primary focus 
must be on exchanges (in which term I include statements and 
conduct) which cross the line between the defendant and the 
plaintiﬀ.” 
(Lord Steyn, Williams v Natural Life Health Foods [1998] 1 WLR 
830, p.835) 
LAW OF TORT
B. An objective test
“In these cases in which the loss has been caused by the claimant’s 
reliance on information provided by the defendant, it is critical to 
decide whether the defendant (rather than someone else) assumed 
responsibility for the accuracy of the information to the claimant 
(rather than to someone else) or for its use by the claimant for one 
purpose (rather than another). The answer does not depend upon 
what the defendant intended but … upon what would reasonably be 
inferred from his conduct against the background of all the 
circumstances of the case.” 
(Lord Hoﬀmann, in Customs and Excise Commissioners v Barclays Bank 
plc [2006] UKHL 28, [35])
LAW OF TORT
B. An objective test
“I do not think that voluntary assumption of responsibility is a 
helpful or realistic test for liability. … Obviously, if an adviser 
expressly assumes responsibility for his advice, a duty of care 
will arise, but such is extremely unlikely in the ordinary course 
of events. …[In most cases if D] had actually been asked if he 
was voluntarily assuming responsibility for his advice to the 
mortgagee or the purchaser of the shares, I have little doubt he 
would have replied, ‘Certainly not. My responsibility is limited 
to the person who employs me.’”
(Lord Griﬃths, in Smith v Eric Bush, p.862)

LAW OF TORT
C. Assumption must be 
voluntary
Customs and Excise Commissioners v Barclays Bank plc [2006] 
UKHL 28
“Indeed the bank and [C] are about as far from being in a 
relationship “equivalent to contract” as they could be. The bank 
has to act, but merely because the court order compels it to do so 
ultimately on pain of punishment. It gets its reasonable costs, but 
makes no proﬁt out of performing its duty. Moreover, its duty to 
respect the order and to act accordingly does not arise out of any 
relationship with [C].”
(Lord Rodger, [65])
LAW OF TORT
C. Assumption must be 
voluntary
Customs and Excise Commissioners v Barclays Bank plc [2006] UKHL 
28
“As the law has developed (and in the ﬁeld of pure economic loss) 
liability is imposed because of the voluntary assumption of 
responsibility. But in the modern context the word “voluntary” is being 
used, it seems to me, with the connotation of “conscious”, 
“considered” or “deliberate”. … In this case the appellant bank has not, 
in any meaningful sense, made a voluntary assumption of 
responsibility. It has by the freezing order had responsibility thrust 
upon it.”
(Lord Walker, [73-74])
LAW OF TORT
C. Assumption must be 
voluntary
SO … the assumption does not have to be ‘voluntary’ in a 
strong sense
“A’s decision to buy the last remaining bottle of Dow’s 1871 
Vintage Port from B is still binding even if A has previously 
contracted to sell such a bottle to C, and so has no real choice 
but to enter the transaction.” (Nolan, ‘Assumption of 
Responsibility: Four Questions’)
What is required, is that D undertook a task for C, rather than 
D being forced to do something which would incidentally 
beneﬁt C
LAW OF TORT
D. Not limited to PEL!!
Although the assumption of responsibility doctrine has its 
foundations in Hedley Byrne, which concerned PEL, it is 
not limited to this domain.
As you will see in the coming weeks, the doctrine can be 
the basis for the imposition of liability in lots of other 
situations where there would not be a duty of care 
otherwise – especially omissions cases
The concept is important for understanding the law of 
negligence as a whole
LAW OF TORT
E. Closing thoughts
“At least four diﬀerent schools of thought are discernible in the academic 
writings on assumption of responsibility, …: 
1.
that the concept as employed by the courts is essentially meaningless, and 
either a foil for policy arguments or for an alternative, allegedly more 
meaningful concept, such as ‘proximity’; 
2.
that cases of assumption of responsibility are really just instances of liability 
in contract, minus consideration; 
3.
that in the cases where the assumption of responsibility concept is employed 
no liability should have been imposed at all; and 
4.
that assumption of responsibility is a distinctive sui generis basis of legal 
obligation, separate from both core negligence law and the law of contract.”
(Nolan, ‘Assumption of Responsibility: Four Questions’)
