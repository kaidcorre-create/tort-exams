LAW OF TORT
Legal causation v factual 
causation
Factual causation was about whether D has caused C’s 
damage at all
Legal causation: even if D is a cause of C’s harm does that 
causal relationship justify requiring D to give C a remedy?
• Factual causation must already be proven 
• Which cause the law settles on as being responsible in law is a ‘moral’ choice

1. Remoteness of 
Damage
LAW OF TORT
The history of remoteness
Re Polemis [1921] 3 KB 560
“[I]f the act would or might probably cause damage, the fact 
that the damage it in fact causes is not the exact kind of 
damage one would expect is immaterial, so long as the 
damage is in fact directly traceable to the negligent act, and 
not due to the operation of independent causes … Once the 
act is negligent, the fact that its exact operation was not 
foreseen is immaterial.” (Scrutton LJ, [577]) 
LAW OF TORT
The Wagon Mound (No 1) 
[1961] AC 388 
Facts:
D allowed oil to spill into Sydney Harbour while refueling.
Damage caused by ﬁre not reasonably foreseeable because diﬃcult to ignite a thin ﬁlm of oil on 
water
Lower Courts:
Damage not reasonably foreseeable, but D still liable – under rule in Re Polemis.
House of Lords:
If damage was not of a reasonably foreseeable kind, then D not liable – Re Polemis overruled.
LAW OF TORT
The Wagon Mound (No 1) 
[1961] AC 388 
“it does not seem consonant with current ideas of justice or 
morality that for an act of negligence, however slight or 
venial, which results in some trivial foreseeable damage the 
actor should be liable for all consequences however 
unforeseeable and however grave, so long as they can be said 
to be ‘direct.’” (Viscount Simonds, p.421)
“the essential factor in determining liability is whether the 
damage is of such a kind as the reasonable man should have 
foreseen. This accords with the general view thus stated by 
Lord Atkin in Donoghue” (Viscount Simonds, p.426)
LAW OF TORT
Hughes v Lord Advocate 
[1963] AC 837
Facts:
D left a manhole open during roadworks. 
At night paraﬃn lamps put around it to warn passers-by. 
C, an 8 y.o., accidentally kicked lamp into the hole, causing 
an explosion that injured C. 
LAW OF TORT
Hughes v Lord Advocate 
[1963] AC 837
“[C’s] injuries were mainly caused by burns, and it cannot be 
said that injuries from burns were unforeseeable. … No 
doubt it was not to be expected that the injuries would be as 
serious as those which the appellant in fact sustained. But 
[D] is liable, although the damage may be a good deal 
greater in extent than was foreseeable. He can only escape 
liability if the damage can be regarded as diﬀering in kind 
from what was foreseeable.” 
(Lord Reid, p.845)
LAW OF TORT
Hughes v Lord Advocate 
[1963] AC 837
Held:
D liable.
Even though an explosion was not reasonably foreseeable, 
some type of burn was reasonably foreseeable.
“To my mind, the distinction drawn between burning and 
explosion is too ﬁne to warrant acceptance.” (Lord Jenkins, 
p.850)
LAW OF TORT
Jolley v Sutton LBC [2000] 
1 WLR 1082
Facts:
14-year old boy injured while playing with a disused boat
He crawled underneath the boat which was propped up
Held:
Some danger from meddling with the boat was reasonably 
foreseeable even if the the speciﬁc danger of the boat falling 
was not reasonably foreseeable
D liable
LAW OF TORT
Tremain v Pike [1969] 1 
WLR 1556
Facts:
D negligently allowed his farm to become infested with 
rats
C farm worker contracted Weil’s disease as a result of 
exposure to rat urine
Held:
Danger from a rat biting was reasonably foreseeable, but 
danger from rate urine was not. D not liable.
LAW OF TORT
The eggshell skull rule
Smith v Leech Brain & Co Ltd [1962] 2 QB 405
C was burnt on the lip because of D’s negligence.
C had a pre-cancerous skin condition, which became 
cancerous as a result of the burn, and C eventually died.
D held liable for the full consequences of his negligence – 
D ‘takes the claimant as they ﬁnd them’. 
LAW OF TORT
The eggshell skull rule
Distinction between type of harm and quantity of harm:
D’s liability for type of harm limited by what is reasonably 
foreseeable
D’s liability for quantity of harm not limited by what is 
reasonably foreseeable
Once established that the kind of damage is reasonably 
foreseeable, D is liable for the full extent damage
LAW OF TORT
2. New Intervening Acts
LAW OF TORT
‘DIVU’
An intervening act by C or TP will be more likely to break 
the chain of causation if it is:
Deliberate
Informed
Voluntary
Unreasonable
LAW OF TORT
‘DIVU’
“…where the line is to be drawn is not capable of precise deﬁnition. Various 
considerations may, however, commonly be relevant. In a case involving intervening 
conduct, these may include: 
1.
The extent to which the conduct was reasonably foreseeable — in general, the more 
foreseeable it is, the less likely it is to be a novus actus interveniens. 
2.
The degree of unreasonableness of the conduct— in general, the more unreasonable 
the conduct, the more likely it is to be a novus actus interveniens and a number of cases 
have stressed the need for a high degree of unreasonableness. 
3.
The extent to which it was voluntary and independent conduct— in general, the 
more deliberate the act, the more informed it is and the greater the free choice 
involved, the more likely it is to be a novus actus interveniens.”
(Hamblen LJ in Clay v TUI) 
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
McKew v Holland [1969] 3 ALL ER 1621
Facts:
 C suﬀered an injury caused by D’s negligence. As a result 
his leg occasionally gave way under him.
C descended a steep staircase with no hand rail.
C’s leg gave way and to avoid falling, he threw himself 
forwards, fracturing his ankle.
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
McKew v Holland [1969] 3 ALL ER 1621
Held:
D not liable.
“‘…if the injured man acts unreasonably he cannot hold 
[D] liable for injury caused by his own unreasonable 
conduct. His unreasonable conduct is novus actus 
interveniens. The chain of causation has been broken and 
what follows must be regarded as caused by his own 
conduct and not by [D’s] fault or the disability caused by 
it.” (Lord Reid, [70])
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
McKew v Holland [1969] 3 ALL ER 1621
Although C’s act was reasonably foreseeable, it still broke the chain of 
causation.
“I do not think that foreseeability comes into this. [D] is not liable for a 
consequence of a kind which is not foreseeable. But it does not follow 
that he is liable for every consequence which a reasonable man could 
foresee. …[I]t is often easy to foresee unreasonable conduct or some 
other novus actus interveniens as being quite likely. But that does not 
mean that the defender must pay for damage caused by the novus 
actus.” (Lord Reid, [70])
N.b. Contrary to Hamblen LJ in Clay v TUI
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
Spencer v Wincanton [2009] EWCA Civ 1404
D negligently injured C’s leg. C required prosthetic leg. C 
tried to ﬁll up their car without using crutches or 
prosthetic leg and fell, suﬀering further injury.
C’s conduct was not wholly unreasonable as in McKew. No 
novus actus, but reduction of 1/3 for contributory 
negligence. 
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
Clay v TUI [2018] EWCA Civ 1177
Facts:
C enjoying a package holiday with extended family
C trapped on the balcony for 30 minutes as a result of 
being locked out of the hotel room because of D’s 
negligence
C tried to get from one balcony to another
C fell and suﬀered a skull fracture
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
Clay v TUI [2018] EWCA Civ 1177
Held:
C was not in an emergency – no need for C to take on the 
risk of trying to cross balconies
What C had done was not reasonably foreseeable
D not liable
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
Corr v IBC Vehicles [2008] 1 AC 884 
C’s ear severed by D’s negligence. C became depressed, and took his 
own life. 
“C’s suicide was not a voluntary, informed decision taken by him as 
an adult of sound mind making and giving eﬀect to a personal 
decision about his future. It was the response of a man suﬀering 
from a severely depressive illness which impaired his capacity to 
make reasoned and informed judgments about his future, such 
illness being, as is accepted, a consequence of the employer’s tort.” 
(Lord Bingham, [16])
LAW OF TORT
2(a): acts of C
N.b. suicide can be a novus actus if it is DIVU, rather 
than a foreseeable consequence of D’s negligence 
“the police should not owe a person of sound mind a 
duty to take reasonable care to prevent him from 
committing suicide. If he wants to take his life, that 
is his business. He is a responsible human being and 
should accept the intended consequences of his acts 
without blaming anyone else.” 
(Reeves v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis [2000] 1 
AC 360, Lord Hoﬀmann, p.368)
LAW OF TORT
2(b): acts of third party
Lamb v Camden LBC [1981] EWCA Civ 7
Facts:
D’s negligence caused building damage
C moved all the furniture out 
Squatters moved in
Held:
D not liable for damage caused by squatters – the squatting was a novus 
actus
LAW OF TORT
2(b): acts of third party
Lamb v Camden LBC [1981] EWCA Civ 7
Denning LJ:
Whether it was foreseeable that the squatters would move in is irrelevant. The 
issue is purely one of ‘policy’.
As a matter of policy, it was the job of C to keep the squatters out and evict 
them. D should not be liable. 
Watkins LJ:
Reasonable foreseeability should always be the starting point. Only once 
satisﬁed should the court consider policy issues. 
 “We are here dealing with unreasonable conduct of an outrageous kind … 
This kind of anti-social and criminal behaviour provides a glaring example of 
an act which is too remote.”
LAW OF TORT
2(b): acts of third party
Knightley v Johns [1982] 1 WLR 349
Facts:
D1, a driver, caused an accident in a tunnel.
D2, a police chief, ordered C to drive back through the 
tunnel against traﬃc, to seal it oﬀ. 
D3, another driver, hit and injured C.
LAW OF TORT
2(b): acts of third party
Knightley v Johns [1982] 1 WLR 349
Held:
What D2 did was not ordinary, but gross negligence. He 
violated police codes. 
So, despite the emergency context, D2’s act broke the chain 
of causation. D2 liable, not D1.
