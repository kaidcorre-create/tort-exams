LAW OF TORT
Overview
Standard but-for causation: D liable
Material contribution to divisible injury: D liable for the 
part of the injury they caused
Material contribution to indivisible injury: D liable in full
Material contribution to the risk of injury: D liable in 
proportion to their contribution to the risk
LAW OF TORT
Tea scenario #1 (material 
contribution to divisible 
injury)
D1, D2, and D3 each add a drop of salmonella to C’s tea
Suppose that for each drop of salmonella, C will have 
another week of food poisoning
C drinks the tea and get food poisoning for 3 weeks
Result: each D is liable only for one week of food poisoning, 
as that is what they each caused (standard ‘but-for’)
LAW OF TORT
Tea scenario #2 (material 
contribution to indivisible 
injury)
D1, D2, and D3 each add a drop of deadly poison to C’s tea
Suppose that each drop of poison would, alone, kill C
C drinks the tea and dies
Result: each D is liable entirely for C’s death (departure from 
‘but-for’)
LAW OF TORT
Tea scenario #3 (material 
contribution to risk of 
injury)
D1, D2, and D3 each add a drop of kryptonite to C’s tea
Suppose that each time you drink a drop of kryptonite you 
have a 10% chance of being poisoned. Sometimes you are 
ﬁne, but 10% of the time you die.
C drinks the tea, and dies
Result: D1, D2, and D3 each liable for 1/3 of damages

LAW OF TORT
Wilsher v Essex Area Health 
Authority [1988] 1 AC 1074
D negligently gave C too much oxygen, which increased 
risk of RLF
C developed RLF
There were ﬁve diﬀerent and equally likely possible causes 
of the RLF
Held:
D not liable because no causation
LAW OF TORT
Wilsher distinguished 
“It is one thing to treat an increase of risk as equivalent to the 
making of a material contribution where a single noxious 
agent is involved, but quite another where any one of a 
number of noxious agents may equally probably have caused 
the damage.” (Lord Bingham in Fairchild, [22])
“In my opinion it is an essential condition for the operation 
of the exception that the impossibility of proving that the 
defendant caused the damage arises out of the existence of 
another potential causative agent which operated in the 
same way.” (Lord Hoﬀmann in Barker, [24])
LAW OF TORT
Tea scenario #5
D puts a drop of ‘serum X’ in C’s tea which increases C’s 
chance of food poisoning by 10%
C used some old water in his tea which had ‘serum Y’ in it 
increasing his chance of food poisoning by 10%
C drinks the tea and gets food poisoning
Result: D only liable if serum X and Y cause food poisoning 
in the same way, otherwise not liable

Criticism of MCRI
“In the case of mesothelioma claims, there can be no real 
doubt but if Fairchild had been decided the other way, in 
accordance with normal common law principles, Parliament 
would have intervened very promptly. That may very well 
have been a better solution, but it can fairly be said that that 
observation is made with the wisdom of hindsight.” (Lords 
Neuberger & Reed, Zurich Insurance [2015] UKSC 33, [211])
“[T]his line of authority is very much an exception to the 
normal rule, and … further judicial experimentation in this 
area is not to be encouraged.” (W&J, p.176)
LAW OF TORT
The future
MCRI turns on the scientiﬁc impossibility of knowing 
whether D’s negligence caused C’s injury
If science develops to the point where such knowledge is 
attainable, then MCRI will disappear
E.g. if we can identify exactly which strand of asbestos 
caused mesothelioma and we can date it precisely, then we 
will know which D caused it – so, no need for MCRI 
approach

LAW OF TORT
The setup: loss of a chance
C cannot show on the balance of probabilities that D 
caused C harm, but C can show that D caused C to lose a 
chance of avoiding that harm 
Really a question about whether losing a chance counts as 
‘damage’ rather than whether D has ‘caused’ damage
Problem arises because of ‘causal uncertainty’ — because 
we cannot know for sure what would have happened if D 
had not been negligent
LAW OF TORT
Hotson v East Berkshire 
HA [1987] AC 750
Facts:
C fell from a tree and hurt his hip – D negligently 
misdiagnosed C
If correctly diagnosed there would have been 25% chance 
that C’s hip would have recovered
Negligent diagnosis meant 0% chance of C recovering
LAW OF TORT
Hotson v East Berkshire 
HA [1987] AC 750
Held:
Because C’s hip only had a 25% chance of recovering, on 
the balance of probabilities he would have lost the hip
So, it did not matter that D’s negligence had reduced that 
chance to 0%
No scope for giving 25% damages to C (as HC and COA 
had held below)
LAW OF TORT
Gregg v Scott [2005] 2 AC 
176 
Facts:
D’s negligence delayed C receiving medical treatment for 
cancer 
C initially had a 42% chance of recovery 
D’s negligence had reduced that to a 25% chance 
Held:
D not liable because C had a less than 50% chance of recovery 
in the ﬁrst place
LAW OF TORT
Gregg v Scott [2005] 2 AC 
176 
Lady Hale’s points
1. Allowing damages for lost chances would render ‘but-for’ 
causation redundant, because in almost every case where 
there is a breach of duty this will cause a loss of a chance 
for C to some degree. [215]
2. The gist of negligence is damage, and if we allow recovery 
for lost chances it would do away with the need to show 
damage. [217]
LAW OF TORT
Gregg v Scott [2005] 2 AC 
176 
Lady Hale’s points
3.
If C were compensated for his lost chance, then it would make no sense to 
retain the rule that Cs in tort cases are compensated 100% in normal 
circumstances. Otherwise, we would have a mixed approach where:
a.
Cs who can show their injury was >50% likely to have been caused by D 
get 100% damages, but;
b.
Cs who can show their injury was <50% likely to have been caused by D 
get damages in proportion to their lost chance.
This would make no sense – it would be a “heads you lose everything, tails I 
win something” situation. 
Alternative would be to adopt approach (b) for all cases – but this would 
add a lot of complexity and cost to cases.
LAW OF TORT
Gregg v Scott [2005] 2 AC 
176 
The dissents
Lord Nicholls: The point of a medical duty is to promote 
the patient’s recovery, it would be irrational to empty this 
duty of content. A 45% chance of recovery is ‘real’!
Lord Hope: The delay allowed the cancer to grow, this was 
a physical injury and ‘chance’ could be assessed, as usual, 
through damages.

“There is not much diﬀerence between the money one expected 
to have and the money one expected to have a chance of 
having: it is all money. There is a diﬀerence between the leg one 
ought to have and the chance of keeping a leg which one ought 
to have.” 
(Lady Hale in Gregg, [220])
A chance of money can be valued easily – e.g. you can sell a 
bet

The rule in Allied Maples Group Ltd v Simmons & Simmons 
[1995] 1 WLR 1602
“To the extent (if at all) that the question whether [C] would 
have been better oﬀ depends upon what [C] would have 
done [had D not been negligent], this must be proved by the 
claimant upon the balance of probabilities. To the extent that 
the supposed beneﬁcial outcome depends upon what others 
would have done [had D not been negligent], this depends 
upon a loss of chance evaluation.” (Perry v Raleys Solicitors 
[2019] UKSC 5, [20])

The rule in Allied Maples Group Ltd v Simmons & Simmons 
[1995] 1 WLR 1602
If C can show it is >50% likely they would have done 
something which would get them X amount had D not 
been negligent, then C gets X amount; but if C can only 
show it is <50% likely they would have done that thing, 
then they get 0
Whereas, if C can show it is N% likely that TP would have 
done something which would get C that X amount had D 
not been negligent, then C gets N% of X amount
