LAW OF TORT
A. Intentional act
Iqbal v Prison Oﬃcers Association [2009] EWCA Civ 1312
Facts
C, a prisoner, sued D when prison guards unlawfully went 
on strike which led to C being conﬁned to his cell all day. 
Held
No false imprisonment for three reasons: (i) no intention; 
(ii) no ‘act’; and (iii) no directness.
LAW OF TORT
A. Intentional act
Iqbal v Prison Oﬃcers Association [2009] EWCA Civ 1312
“[F]alse imprisonment is diﬀerent from battery. … [W]ith 
false imprisonment, the loss of liberty is the essence of the 
tort and, in my view, the claimant must show not merely an 
intentional act or omission … but also an intention to deprive 
the claimant of his liberty.”
(Smith LJ, [72])
LAW OF TORT
A. Intentional act
Iqbal v Prison Oﬃcers Association [2009] EWCA Civ 1312
“If a security guard in an oﬃce block locks the door to the 
claimant's room believing that the claimant has gone home for 
the night and not realising that he is in fact still inside the 
room, he has committed a deliberate act. However, he did not 
intend to conﬁne the claimant. He may well be guilty of 
negligence because he did not check whether the room was 
empty but he would not be guilty of the intentional tort of false 
imprisonment.” 
(Smith LJ, [72])
LAW OF TORT
A. Intentional act
Iqbal v Prison Oﬃcers Association [2009] EWCA Civ 1312
“the mere failure of the prison oﬃcers to work at the 
prison, while it may have been a breach of their 
employment contracts, involved no positive action on their 
part.”(Neuberger LJ, [34])
“the general rule that an omission or refusal to release the 
claimant from conﬁnement will not amount to false 
imprisonment” (Smith LJ, [61])
LAW OF TORT
B. Directness
Davidson v Chief Constable of North Wales [1994] 2 All ER 597
Facts
D was a store detective, who wrongly thought Cs were stealing from the store.
D called the police and Cs were arrested and held for 2 hours.
Held
Distinction between: (a) D who makes a complaint or report to a police oﬃcer, leaving 
the latter to make up his mind whether to make an arrest and, (b) D who actually 
instigates the arrest, instructing or authorising the police oﬃcer to act. → (a) is not 
false imprisonment, but (b) is.
Here, what D did was (a), so not false imprisonment.
LAW OF TORT
B. Directness
Iqbal v Prison Oﬃcers Association [2009] EWCA Civ 1312
“In the present case the prison oﬃcers simply withdrew their 
labour, which did not directly lead to the claimant being conﬁned 
in his cell … The strike may have caused, indeed foreseeably 
caused, the governor to decide not to let the claimant out of his 
cell … but that is a diﬀerent thing. … [I]t is equally true that it 
must have appeared likely, indeed almost inevitable, to the 
defendant store detective in the Davidson case … that, once she 
laid credible information before a police constable that she had 
just seen [C] shoplifting, the constable would arrest [C].”
(Neuberger LJ, [27])
LAW OF TORT
C. Complete restriction of 
liberty
Bird v Jones [1845] 7 QB 742 
Facts
D had constructed a seating enclosure on a bridge and stationed policemen who prevented C 
passing through the area for 30 minutes. 
Held 
No false imprisonment.
‘A prison must have its boundaries large or narrow … it may be movable or ﬁxed, but a 
boundary it must have’. 
‘imprisonment is … a total restraint of the liberty of the person … and not a partial obstruction of 
his will, whatever inconvenience it may bring on him’
LAW OF TORT
C. Complete restriction of 
liberty
Burton v Davies (1953) (Australia) 
Facts
D groped C in a speeding car.
Held
False imprisonment as it was too fast to safely get out.
Just because there are technically means of escaping, it is still false 
imprisonment if the means of escape is dangerous, inconvenient, or 
unreasonable.
LAW OF TORT
C. Complete restriction of 
liberty
Herd v Weardale Steel Coke & Coal  [1915] AC 67
Facts
C refused to work because of what he saw as unsafe conditions 
at the start of a mining shift. He wasn’t taken back up until 
hours later. 
Held
No false imprisonment. C accepted as part of his contract that 
he would be underground until the end of the shift. 
LAW OF TORT
C. Complete restriction of 
liberty
Robinson v Balmain New Ferry [1910] AC 295 
Facts
C paid to enter D’s wharf in order to use a ferry. He missed it. A sign by the 
entry turnstile noted that there was a fee to exit in all events. Refusing to pay, 
C was held until he forced his way out. 
Held
C had contracted that to avoid payment he would leave the wharf by a 
diﬀerent route.
No false imprisonment. 
LAW OF TORT
C. Complete restriction of 
liberty
Meering v Grahame-White Aviation (1920) 122 LT 44
“a person could be imprisoned without his knowing it. I 
think a person can be imprisoned while he is asleep, while he 
is in a state of drunkenness, while he is unconscious, and 
while he is a lunatic … Of course the damages might be 
diminished and would be aﬀected by the question whether 
he was conscious of it or not.” 
(Atkin LJ, [53-54])
LAW OF TORT
D. No lawful excuse
R (Lumba) v Secretary of State of the Home Department [2011] 
UKSC 12 
Facts
C was detained after a prison sentence until a decision on their 
deportation under an unpublished secret policy. 
C sued for false imprisonment arguing that the secret policy 
was illegal. 
D argued that, in fact, C probably would have been detained 
under a lawful policy anyway.
LAW OF TORT
D. No lawful excuse
R (Lumba) v Secretary of State of the Home Department [2011] UKSC 
12 
Held
1.
The secret policy was unlawful as a matter of public law.
2.
Minor public law unlawfulness of detention ≠ false imprisonment.
3.
Serious public law unlawfulness of detention = false imprisonment.
4.
Here, the decision to detain was tainted by serious public law error. C 
had been falsely imprisoned.
5.
However, the fact that C could have been lawfully detained under 
another, lawful policy, meant C was only awarded nominal damages.

LAW OF TORT
A. Consent
Blake v Galloway [2004] 1 WLR 2844
Facts
C and D, teenagers, were engaging in some ‘horseplay’ throwing small 
pieces of bark at one another. 
C was injured and sued in battery. 
Held
C had impliedly consented to the battery by participating in the 
‘horseplay’. 
LAW OF TORT
A. Consent
Blake v Galloway [2004] 1 WLR 2844
“In a sport which inevitably involves the risk of some physical 
contact, the participants are taken impliedly to consent to those 
contacts which can reasonably be expected to occur in the course 
of the game, and to assume the risk of injury from such contacts. 
Thus, for example, in the context of a ﬁght with ﬁsts, ordinarily 
neither party has a cause of action for any injury suﬀered during 
the ﬁght. But they do not assume ‘the risk of a savage blow out of 
all proportion to the occasion.’” 
(Dyson LJ, [21]) 
LAW OF TORT
A. Consent
Chatterton v Gerson [1981] 1 All ER 257
Facts
C underwent an operation and suﬀered from a debilitating side eﬀect 
of the operation. 
C sued in battery, arguing that D had failed to tell her about the 
inherent risks in the operation and so her consent was vitiated. 
Held
As she had generally understood the operation her consent was not 
vitiated. 
LAW OF TORT
A. Consent
Chatterton v Gerson [1981] 1 All ER 257
“once the patient is informed in broad terms of the nature 
of the procedure which is intended, and gives her consent, 
that consent is real, and the cause of the action on which to 
base a claim for failure to go into risks and implications is 
negligence, not trespass [i.e. battery]”. (Bristow J, p.443)
(Compare Montgomery v Lanarkshire – failure to inform C 
of risks of medical treatment is a breach of duty in 
negligence.)
LAW OF TORT
A. Consent
Chatterton v Gerson [1981] 1 All ER 257
“Of course if information is withheld in bad faith, the 
consent will be vitiated by fraud. Of course if by some 
accident, as in a case in the 1940's in the Salford Hundred 
Court where a boy was admitted to hospital for tonsilectomy 
and due to administrative error was circumcised instead, 
trespass [i.e. battery] would be the appropriate cause of 
action against the doctor… .”
(Bristow J, p.443)

B. Necessity
F v West Berkshire Health Authority [1989] 2 AC 1
Facts
C was a patient  in a mental hospital who lacked capacity – 
mental age of a child.
C began a sexual relationship with another patient, and 
hospital staﬀ and her mother feared she would become 
pregnant, which she would be unable to handle.
Her mother sought to have her sterilised. 
LAW OF TORT
B. Necessity
F v West Berkshire Health Authority [1989] 2 AC 1
Held
C lacked capacity, and sterilisation was in her best 
interests.
It would not, therefore, be a battery for doctors to sterilise 
C.
LAW OF TORT
B. Necessity
F v West Berkshire Health Authority [1989] 2 AC 1
“…the basic requirements, applicable in these cases of 
necessity ...”
(1)“a necessity to act when it is not practicable to communicate 
with the assisted person, but also 
(2)the action taken must be such as a reasonable person would in 
all the circumstances take, acting in the best interests of the 
assisted person.” 
(Lord Goﬀ, [75])
LAW OF TORT
B. Necessity
Mental Capacity Act 2005 
s2(1)
“a person lacks capacity in relation to a matter if at the material time he is unable to make a decision for 
himself in relation to the matter because of an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the 
mind or brain.”
s3(1)
“For the purposes of section 2, a person is unable to make a decision for himself if he is unable—  
(a) to understand the information relevant to the decision,  
(b) to retain that information,  
(c) to use or weigh that information as part of the process of making the decision, or  
(d) to communicate his decision (whether by talking, using sign language or any 
other means).”
LAW OF TORT
C. Self-defence
Ashley v Chief Constable of Sussex [2008] UKHL 25
Facts
Cs were related to X, who had been shot dead by the police during a raid. 
The oﬃcer had been criminally acquitted on the basis of self-defence as he 
honestly believed himself to be at threat of attack from X. 
Held
In tort, D’s belief must not just be honest but also reasonable.
D’s belief was not reasonable – so he was held liable in battery.
LAW OF TORT
C. Self-defence
Ashley v Chief Constable of Sussex [2008] UKHL 25
“It is one thing to say that if A’s mistaken belief was honestly 
held he should not be punished by the criminal law. It would 
be quite another to say that A’s unreasonably held mistaken 
belief would be suﬃcient to justify the law in setting aside 
B’s [civil] right not to be subjected to physical violence by 
A.”
(Lord Scott, [18])
LAW OF TORT
C. Self-defence
D’s actions must be proportionate to the threat for the 
defence to succeed.
Compare:
Cockcroft v Smith [1705] – C’s act of running with his ﬁnger 
extended towards the D’s eyes did not justify D oﬀ part of 
the ﬁnger!!
Cross v Kirkby [2000] – Where D acts reasonably in self-
defence, “the law does not require him to measure the 
violence to be deployed with mathematical precision” 
(Judge LJ, [90])
