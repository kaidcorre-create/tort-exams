History
LAW OF TORT
The early days
Negligence was ‘fragmented’ – a series of speciﬁc duties in speciﬁc 
contexts 
• Gladwell v Steggall (1839) – Duty of doctor to patient
• Smith v Baker & Sons (1891) – Duty of employer to employee
• Blacker v Lake & Elliot (1912) – D not liable when he sold product to A which then, because of defect, 
injured C
“It is remarkable how diﬃcult it is to ﬁnd in the English authorities 
statements of general application deﬁning the relations between 
parties that give rise to the duty. The Courts are concerned with the 
particular relations which come before them in actual litigation, and 
it is suﬃcient to say whether the duty exists in those circumstances.” 
(Lord Atkin, in Donoghue)
LAW OF TORT
The fundamental 
breakthrough: Donoghue
“You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions 
which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure 
your neighbour. Who, then, in law is my neighbour? The 
answer seems to be — persons who are so closely and 
directly aﬀected by my act that I ought reasonably to have 
them in contemplation as being so aﬀected when I am 
directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called 
in question.” 
(Lord Atkin)
LAW OF TORT
The fundamental 
breakthrough: Donoghue
The dissents:
“the common law … though its principles are capable of 
application to meet new conditions not contemplated 
when the law was laid down … cannot be changed nor can 
additions be made to them because any particular 
meritorious case seems outside their ambit.” (Lord 
Buckmaster)
“If one step, why not ﬁfty?” (Lord Buckmaster)
LAW OF TORT
The ‘established 
category’ from Donoghue
To this day, Dongohue and the cases immediately after it 
stand as good authority for the following propositions:
You have duty not to do negligent acts which will 
reasonably foreseeably cause physical injury to others.
You have duty not to do negligent acts which will 
reasonably foreseeably cause damage to others’ property.

The era of expansion
“[W]hen a new point emerges, one should ask not whether it 
is covered by authority but whether recognised principles 
apply to it. Donoghue … may be regarded as a milestone, and 
the well known passage in Lord Atkin's speech should I

(Dorset Yacht Co Ltd v Home Oﬃce [1970] UKHL 2, per Lord

LAW OF TORT
The era of expansion
The “two stage test” from Anns v Merton LBC [1977] UKHL 
4:
1. Could D reasonably foresee that their negligence would 
cause damage to C? If ‘yes’, then a prima facie duty of 
care arises.
2. If yes, are there any considerations which negate, reduce, 
or limit the scope of the duty? If ‘no’, then there is a duty.
LAW OF TORT
The era of expansion
The high-water mark: Junior Books Ltd v Veitchi Co Ltd 
[1983] 1 AC 520 — duty of care extended to pure economic 
loss arising from defective property! 
But, everyone (mostly) agreed things had gone too far:
Anns overruled by Caparo in 1990
Junior Books overruled in 1991 by Murhpy Brentwood DC
LAW OF TORT
Reining it in: Caparo v 
Dickman [1990] 2 AC 605 
“in addition to the foreseeability of damage, necessary 
ingredients in any situation giving rise to a duty of care are 
that there should exist between the party owing the duty 
and the party to whom it is owed a relationship 
characterised by the law as one of 'proximity' or 
'neighbourhood' and that the situation should be one in 
which the court considers it fair, just and reasonable that 
the law should impose a duty of a given scope upon the 
one party for the beneﬁt of the other.” (Lord Bridge)
LAW OF TORT
Reining it in: Caparo v 
Dickman [1990] 2 AC 605 
But Caparo did not intend to create a “general test”!
“[Foreseeability, proximity, and fairness, justice & 
reasonableness] amount in eﬀect to little more than convenient 
labels to attach to the features of diﬀerent speciﬁc situations

(Lord Bridge)
“the law should develop novel categories of negligence

(Lord Bridge, citing Brennan J. in the High Ct of Australia)

Trying to get back to 
incrementalism
“In Caparo … Lord Bridge …. emphasised the inability of any 
single general principle to provide a practical test which could 
be applied to every situation to determine whether a duty of 
care is owed and, if so, what is its scope. … Paradoxically, this 
passage in Lord Bridge’s speech has sometimes come to be

LAW OF TORT
The current approach 
(again): Robinson
“The proposition that there is a Caparo test which applies to all 
claims in the modern law of negligence, and that in consequence the 
court will only impose a duty of care where it considers it fair, just 
and reasonable to do so on the particular facts, is mistaken … [T]he 
whole point of the Caparo case … was to repudiate the idea that there 
is a single test which can be applied in all cases in order to 
determine whether a duty of care exists, and instead to adopt an 
approach based, in the manner characteristic of the common law, on 
precedent, and on the development of the law incrementally and by 
analogy with established authorities.” 
(Lord Reed, [21])

LAW OF TORT
Aﬃrming Robinson: N v 
Poole BC [2019] UKSC 25 
“[Robinson] explained, as Michael had previously done, 
that Caparo did not impose a universal tripartite test for the 
existence of a duty of care, but recommended an incremental 
approach to novel situations, based on the use of established 
categories of liability as guides, by analogy, to the existence and 
scope of a duty of care in cases which fall outside them. The 
question whether the imposition of a duty of care would be fair, 
just and reasonable forms part of the assessment of whether such 
an incremental step ought to be taken. It follows that, in the 
ordinary run of cases, courts should apply established principles 
of law, rather than basing their decisions on their assessment of 
the requirements of public policy.” [64]

LAW OF TORT
Back to the early days?
Does all of this take us back to the original position, where 
the courts focused on speciﬁc duties in speciﬁc contexts, 
rather than general principles? Not quite:
The era of expansion means that there are now many more 
established categories than there were in the early days.
In the early days it was just established categories, and no 
general principle. Now we have a mix: we begin with 
established categories, and turn to principle where things 
are unclear.
